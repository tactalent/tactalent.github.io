GPT-6 Astra Is a Native Visuo-Tactile Policy for Contact-Rich Manipulation

Key conclusions

  • Contact-rich manipulation is not a moat for specialist models. Astra achieves mean success rates of 93.8% on UniVTAC and 81.8% on OpenViTac, substantially outperforming the reported specialist baselines in our comparison.
  • Touch is not exclusive to specialist models. Without task-specific training, Astra directly interprets and uses tactile images, achieving better overall performance than the reported specialist VTLA baselines in our comparison.
  • Vision can partially compensate for missing tactile information. Vision-only Astra achieves 100% on three texture-related tasks, revealing subtle visual cues underused by specialist models and easily overlooked by human observers.
  • Vision-based tactile sensing is a promising direction. Image representations of contact connect directly to general visual capabilities and may scale alongside foundation models.
  • Parts of current benchmarks are approaching saturation. More challenging contact-rich evaluations should control visual shortcuts and better distinguish the capabilities of general-purpose models.

01 / Overview

Is contact-rich manipulation still a moat for specialist policies?

Studies including GPT-as-Policy, RoboProbe, RoboDawn and RoboICL⁠[1–7] explore general multimodal models as robot policies: models generate actions from observations, instructions and demonstrations, then revise their decisions through interaction. This offers an alternative to task-specific policy training and raises a fundamental question: what underpins the advantages of specialist models?

Contact-rich manipulation is an important candidate. Precise insertion, stable grasping and material discrimination require more than recognizing objects and their positions: they involve physical information such as contact geometry, slip and material properties. Touch is therefore an important source of sensory information for these tasks. Can general models directly use visual and tactile information to perform contact-rich manipulation, or do these tasks still require specially trained policies?

We investigate this question on UniVTAC⁠[8] and OpenViTac⁠[9]. Using RoboICL⁠[4], we provide GPT-6 Astra with a few demonstrations and compare vision-only and visuo-tactile inputs. We ask two questions: can Astra directly use vision-based tactile images to perform contact-rich manipulation? How well can it perform using vision alone?

We evaluate Astra on nineteen simulated tasks from UniVTAC⁠[8] and OpenViTac⁠[9]. Adopted by multiple tactile-policy studies, UniVTAC offers an established evaluation setting with accumulated baselines across eight tasks spanning grasping, placement, classification and precise insertion. The recently introduced OpenViTac includes eleven tasks that further cover physical-property discrimination and precise contact manipulation; its corresponding simulated and real-world tasks, together with the strong correlation between their evaluation results, support the quality of its evaluation framework. We use a 10% stratified sample with sampling seed 42: the sample includes 80 UniVTAC and 130 OpenViTac episodes, including 30 episodes across the three Insert Block v2 shapes.

Our control pipeline builds on RoboICL⁠[4], which organizes demonstrations and online interaction history into observation–action–outcome–observation sequences. This allows a general-purpose model to follow demonstrations and use execution feedback for closed-loop control. We use GPT-6 Astra with xhigh reasoning effort, without training a task-specific policy.

One-shot tasks use 12 segments from a single demonstration. Tasks with distinct material classes or decision branches use two demonstrations with five segments each; Insert Block v2 uses demonstrations of three shapes. Segment length H is selected with reference to task trajectory length and held fixed within each task: 5 by default, 10 for Lift Bottle, Put Bottle, Empty-Can Selection, Grasp Chip, Gear Assembly and Insert Block v1 and v2, and 4 for Grasp Classify. The selected demonstrations remain available throughout the episode.

Within this framework, we compare vision-only (V) and visuo-tactile (V+T) input. V uses visual images alone, while V+T additionally provides left and right tactile RGB images; both conditions use the same demonstrations and evaluation scenes. Camera selection for each task strictly follows the original benchmark’s official configuration. Alongside images, Astra receives robot proprioception and task text, and outputs seven-dimensional actions specifying end-effector translation, rotation and a gripper target.

Some original Grasp Chip success conditions impose unnecessarily strict constraints on execution details. We therefore simplify these conditions: an episode succeeds when the chip remains intact and is grasped, transferred and placed in the target region. All other tasks retain the official success criteria.

03 / Results

Overall performance and visuo-tactile capabilities

3.1 Astra outperforms specialist policies by a wide margin on contact-rich tasks

With visuo-tactile input, Astra achieves mean success rates of 93.8% on UniVTAC and 81.8% on OpenViTac. These exceed the strongest reported baselines in our tables—SimpleTouch at 70.9% and OpenVTLA at 68.7%—by 22.9 and 13.1 percentage points. Vision-only Astra reaches 76.3% and 80.6%, also exceeding the highest baseline mean in each benchmark. Contact-rich manipulation therefore does not constitute an exclusive domain of capability for specialist models.

3.2 Astra demonstrates strong capabilities in interpreting and using tactile information

As shown in Figure 2, adding touch improves success rates on six tasks. On UniVTAC, Pull-out Key rises from 30% to 90%, Lift Can from 80% to 100%, Insert Hole from 60% to 100%, and Insert HDMI from 50% to 80%. On OpenViTac, Hardness Classify improves from 70% to 90%, and Insert Block v1 from 40% to 70%. Spanning contact constraints, grasping, precise insertion and material-property judgments, these gains show that Astra can effectively use tactile information in few-shot control without additional tactile-policy training. However, vision-only and visuo-tactile inputs yield equal success rates on several tasks, including Grasp Classify, Roughness Classify and Roughness-Guided Regrasp. Adding touch even reduces success rates on Weight Classify, Empty-Can Selection, Insert Tube and Insert Block v2. Section 3.3 examines possible explanations for these observations.

3.3 Astra’s visual perception can partially compensate for missing tactile information

The two benchmarks show different tactile gains: adding touch improves mean success by 17.5 percentage points on UniVTAC, but by 1.2 points on OpenViTac. This difference may reflect their respective design priorities. UniVTAC emphasizes evaluating the utility of touch and restricts certain visual cues to reduce opportunities for solving tasks through vision alone; most tasks, for example, omit the wrist camera. OpenViTac places greater emphasis on alignment with real-world manipulation, providing both fixed and wrist cameras across all tasks and preserving richer visual information. For Astra, these cues may partially compensate for missing tactile information and support judgments otherwise associated with touch, reducing the additional benefit of tactile input.

Grasp Classify, Roughness Classify and Roughness-Guided Regrasp illustrate this effect: vision-only success is 100% on all three. Audited Grasp Classify frames show visible surface texture on the rough cylinder. In Roughness Classify, the wrist camera reveals ridges and a jagged silhouette on rough objects. In Roughness-Guided Regrasp, surface ridges are discernible even from the fixed camera, allowing Astra to select the rough object before contact. The policy’s action notes also refer to these visual differences.

Astra demonstrates visual discrimination not evident in previously reported specialist-model results, uncovering subtle texture, contour and motion cues that human observers may overlook. These cues partially compensate for missing tactile information. The examples suggest that its sensitivity to particular visual details may even exceed that of human observers. Designing “tactile tasks” therefore requires more than judging whether their visual cues are readily apparent to people. As several tasks approach perfect scores, future benchmarks should control visual cues that can compensate for missing tactile information and introduce visually unobservable physical properties, tighter manipulation tolerances, and more complex contact and recovery sequences to distinguish models’ tactile understanding and manipulation capabilities.

The comparisons below show three classification and selection tasks with 100% success under both vision-only and visuo-tactile inputs. Key visual cues in the action notes are highlighted in green and key tactile cues in red, showing the evidence Astra uses to make judgments under each input condition.

Analysis of tasks whose success rates decreased with tactile input reveals misinterpretation of weak load cues and repeated probing that consumed the action budget, alongside possible stochastic variation. In the three paired Weight Classify cases where vision alone succeeded and visuo-tactile input failed, Astra interpreted small tactile shear or contact patterns resembling the light-object reference as evidence of a light load, misclassifying heavy objects. The vision-only runs instead reached the correct judgment from visible slip after relaxing the grasp. In Empty-Can Selection, subtle tactile changes likewise led to incorrect weight judgments, while repeated comparisons consumed the action budget and left some transfers incomplete. These cases identify the interpretation of weak tactile cues and the efficiency of probing as important factors in performance.

3.4 Astra still has limitations

The preceding examples demonstrate Astra’s perceptual and manipulation capabilities, but these capabilities remain inconsistent under complex contact conditions. Four limitations emerge: limited sensitivity to tangential motion and forces can lead to misinterpretation of subtle tactile cues; fine spatial relationships can be estimated inaccurately; plausible corrective intentions may not translate into effective actions; and repeated probing does not necessarily produce reliable convergence.

The following three visuo-tactile failures illustrate difficulties in physical-property inference, fine alignment and correction, and active probing. Together with the successful examples, they show that the challenge extends beyond proposing a plausible strategy to estimating contact state accurately and translating that judgment into reliable actions.

05 / Conclusion

Toward general-purpose contact manipulation

Contact-rich manipulation and tactile perception are not inherent moats for specialist policies. With only a few demonstrations, GPT-6 Astra directly uses vision-based tactile images to outperform the reported specialist baselines in our comparison across two benchmarks, while exhibiting active probing, fine adjustment and manipulation recovery. Its vision-only performance also reveals subtle cues that human observers may overlook, demonstrating how vision can partially compensate for missing tactile information. Yet interpreting tangential motion and forces, estimating spatial relationships and correcting motion under contact constraints remain inconsistent. Future work should incorporate vision-based tactile sensing into joint pretraining with visual inputs and explore how these capabilities scale alongside foundation models. Tasks involving physical properties that cannot be distinguished visually, tighter manipulation tolerances and real-robot experiments will help establish whether these capabilities translate into reliable physical interaction.
